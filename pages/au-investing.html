Australian Investing Analysis
Two questions evidence-based Australian investors should know: how much can you safely withdraw in retirement, and how concentrated are the market's returns?
The 4% rule came from the 1994 Bengen study on US data. Australia has a different market history, different inflation, different tax treatment (franking credits), and different super rules. This tool runs the same historical sequence-of-returns test on Australian-dollar returns from 1970 onwards (or back to 1901 if you choose — with caveats) across different portfolio mixes: take a starting withdrawal, raise it with inflation every year, and see in how many historical periods the money lasted.

Why Does Australia Differ From the 4% Rule?
✅ Helps
Franking credits are worth about 1.3% a year of portfolio value for 100% Australian shares (assuming a 4% dividend yield, 75% franked) — not in index returns but real in your pocket. How much you keep depends on your tax rate: all of it at 0% (e.g. super in pension phase), about 0.9% a year at 30% and 0.7% at 45%. For a VDHG-like mix (~36% Australian shares) it's about a third of that. The tool applies today's rules to every year (imputation only began in 1987, and cash refunds in 2000).

Long data record: Australian share and inflation data go back to 1901, through two World Wars, the Great Depression (Australian shares about −28% in 1930, a reconstruction), the 1970s stagflation (CPI 15% in 1974–75) and the GFC. The default uses 1970 onwards, when reliable data exist for every asset class; that gives 27 thirty-year periods — a useful test, but not a large sample.
⚠️ Complicates
Home bias risk: AU is ~2% of world market cap. A 100% AU portfolio has high concentration in banks and miners. A bad decade for iron ore or housing could devastate returns.

Super rules change: Preservation age, contribution caps, tax treatment in accumulation vs pension phase — the rules since 1992 are a short history. Pre-super SWR analysis models private portfolios, not exactly how Australians actually retire.

Sequence risk is real: with 100% Australian shares, someone retiring at the start of 1970 could only have withdrawn about 3.9% a year (rising with inflation) for 30 years; someone retiring in 1983 could have taken about 12%. The first decade's returns and inflation largely decide the outcome.

Recent retirees aren't in the test yet: a 30-year period needs 30 years of results, so the latest start year counted is 1996. Retirements that began in 2000 or 2008 are still running.
💡 Takeaways
• Using 1970 onwards (27 thirty-year periods, after fees, franking at a 30% tax rate), a 4% inflation-adjusted withdrawal lasted 30 years in every period for a VDHG-like mix and in 26 of 27 for 100% Australian shares (the exception: retiring in 1970). The heat map above is calculated live for your settings.

• Longer retirements need lower rates: over 40 years, 4% lasted in 16 of 17 periods for a VDHG-like mix (a small sample).

• Monte Carlo is harsher: shuffling historical years at random (which ignores the tendency of markets to recover after crashes) gives a 90%-safe rate of about 3.8% for VDHG over 30 years, versus about 4.85% from the actual historical sequences. A sensible plan sits between the two.

• Franking credits lift effective returns for Australian-share-heavy portfolios; the benefit is largest for 0–15% taxpayers and smallest at 45%.

• Flexible spending (cutting withdrawals after bad years) is widely found to improve survival rates; this tool models fixed real withdrawals only.

• The worst starting years are 1970–1973: shares fell sharply in 1970 and 1973–74 while CPI inflation of 9–15% a year (1973–77) pushed up the cost of withdrawals. History is one sample path — future returns can be worse than anything in this dataset.

Bessembinder (2018) found that over 1926–2016, the best-performing 4% of US-listed stocks (about 1,000 of ~26,000) accounted for all of the net wealth creation above one-month Treasury bills, and just 86 stocks accounted for half of it. The other 96% collectively matched T-bills, and more than half of all stocks returned less than T-bills over their lifetimes. This has profound implications for stock-picking vs diversification. What would this look like for the Australian market?
What the research says about Australia
A 2023 follow-up study (Bessembinder, Chen, Choi & Wei: 63,785 stocks in 42 markets, 1990–2020) does include Australia, and unlike any free dataset it includes companies that were later delisted. It doesn't publish an Australian "top X% created all the wealth" figure, but it does report these Australian results:
Australian stocks that beat US T-bills over the long run
36.1%
So roughly 2 in 3 did worse than cash. Across all markets: 43.2%. The authors cite Australia, with Greece (25.5%), as one of the lowest.
Median Australian stock's buy-and-hold return
−43.2%
The typical Australian listed company lost almost half its value over the period it was in the sample (in US$). Global median: −6.8%.
Gross wealth created by the top 1% of Australian firms
>60%
Australia is one of the markets where the authors say the top 1% of firms accounted for more than sixty percent (exact figure not given in the text).
Global benchmark
2.4%
of all 63,785 firms (1,526 companies) created all of the US$75.7 trillion of net wealth above T-bills, 1990–2020.
Inferred Australian equivalent (approximate): on every measure the study reports, Australia is more lopsided than the global average: fewer stocks beat cash, and the typical stock did much worse. So it's reasonable to infer that, as with the global 2.4%, only a very small slice of ASX-listed companies, likely a few percent at most, created all of the market's net wealth above cash over 1990–2020, with a handful of giants doing most of the work.

Why this is approximate: it's an inference from the study's Australian results, not a figure the authors published. Returns are in US dollars (so the A$ exchange rate matters) and are compared with US Treasury bills, not Australian cash, and the period is 1990–2020 only. A precise ASX-only figure needs the full history of every listed and delisted company, which isn't freely available, so no ASX chart is drawn here rather than one built only from survivors.

Return Distribution — ASX Stocks
The distribution of individual stock returns is highly right-skewed (a few massive winners, many moderate losers). This is the mathematical reason why diversification works: you need to hold ALL of the market to ensure you capture the rare big winners.
What This Means for Australian Investors
📈 For ETF Investors
Owning an index fund guarantees you hold all of the rare massive winners like CBA, CSL and REA Group. A stock picker who held these names correctly would look like a genius — but they also needed to avoid the delisted failures.

Bessembinder's result doesn't mean stocks are bad — it means that diversification across all stocks is essential to reliably capturing the market's positive skew. Holding 10–20 AU stocks dramatically increases the chance you miss the tiny group that accounts for most returns.
☠️ Survivorship Bias Warning
The most dangerous version of this analysis uses only current index constituents — today's ASX 200. These stocks already survived. The real Bessembinder analysis requires including every company ever listed on the ASX, including delisted failures (ABC Learning, HIH Insurance, Allco Finance, Centro, MFS, etc.) — thousands of companies have listed and delisted over the decades.

With survivorship bias removed, the concentration effect is more extreme than a survivors-only analysis would suggest — which is what the 2023 study's Australian figures show.
📚 Comparison with US Findings
Bessembinder's original finding (1926–2016): the top 4% of stocks (~1,000 companies) → 100% of net wealth creation above T-bills; the top 86 stocks → about half of it. A follow-up study of global markets (Bessembinder, Chen, Choi & Wei, 2023) found similarly extreme concentration outside the US.

For Australia, the same study found the effect is stronger than average (36.1% beat T-bills vs 43.2% globally; median −43.2% vs −6.8%). The paper's main text doesn't list which ASX companies created the most wealth, so none are ranked here.
